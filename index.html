Your body is busy

📖 Read

Every spot you tapped is a chemical reaction. A chemical reaction is when one substance is changed into a different substance.

All the chemical reactions in your body, added together, are called metabolism.

Some reactions break big molecules into small ones, like starch into sugar. These are catabolic reactions. Remember it as Catabolic = Cut.

Some reactions build big molecules from small ones, like protein for muscle. These are anabolic reactions. Remember it as Anabolic = Add on.

Try it: chew a plain cracker or a piece of bread for a full minute without swallowing. Does it start to taste a little sweet? That is the reaction from the mouth spot, happening on your tongue.

📖 One more thing

Here is the question: what makes all these reactions happen so fast?

Starch left in a bowl of water would take years to turn into sugar by itself. In your mouth, it starts in seconds. Something in your body is helping. Let's meet it.

📖 Read

That wingman is an enzyme. An enzyme is a protein that speeds up a chemical reaction in your body.

Anything that speeds up a reaction without being used up is called a catalyst. So enzymes are biological catalysts.

Did you notice that the same two wingmen helped pair after pair? Enzymes are not used up. Each one works again and again, thousands of times a second.

And the wingman worked both ways: joining (building up, anabolic) and splitting (breaking down, catabolic).

👀 Try it

So how does an enzyme make a reaction faster?

Every reaction has to get over a hill of energy before it can happen. Give the ball a push. Then switch the enzyme on and push again.

Same push every time. Try it without the enzyme first.
📖 Read

Before any reaction can happen, the molecules need a starting amount of energy. This is called the activation energy. It is the hill the reaction must climb.

An enzyme lowers the activation energy.

The start and the finish stay exactly the same. The enzyme only makes the path easier, so the reaction happens much faster.

Talking to someone new has an awkward hill to climb. A good wingman lowers the awkwardness. He does not change who the two people are. He just makes it easier for them to meet.

Part 3

Shape matters

🤔 Guess first

This enzyme works on only one of these three molecules. Which one do you think it is?

📖 Read

The pocket on the enzyme is called the active site.

The molecule that the enzyme works on is called the substrate.

The substrate fits because its shape is complementary to the active site.

Complementary means the two shapes fit together exactly, like two puzzle pieces. A shape that is nearly right is not good enough. That is why only one molecule worked.

Our wingman is picky. He only works for the one pair that fits.

👀 Watch

Now watch the whole reaction in slow motion.

📖 Read

When the substrate is sitting in the active site, the two together are called the enzyme–substrate complex.

While they are together, the substrate is changed into new substances called products. The products leave, and the enzyme is ready for the next substrate.

This idea is called the lock and key model. The active site is the lock. The substrate is the key.

A crowded fete

🤔 Guess first

👀 Experiment

Speed: 0 reactions per second Busy wingmen: 0/3
Add substrate and watch the speed.
📖 Read

Molecules are always moving and bumping into each other. A reaction can only happen when a substrate bumps into an enzyme's active site. This bump is called a collision.

More substrate means more collisions, so the reaction speeds up. But once every active site is busy, adding more substrate cannot make it go any faster. The enzymes are already working as fast as they can.

Adding more enzyme gives more active sites, so the top speed goes up.

A packed fete means more people bumping into each other. But there are only so many wingmen. When every wingman is busy, the extra people just have to wait their turn.

Part 5

Too hot to work

🤔 Guess first

Our wingman is a protein. What do you think will happen if he gets very, very hot?

👀 Experiment

Slide the temperature slowly from cold to very hot. Watch the enzyme's shape and the speed.

20 °C
📖 Read

Heat makes molecules move faster, so they collide more often. That is why the enzyme speeds up as it warms up.

The temperature where an enzyme works fastest is its optimum temperature. For most enzymes in your body, that is about 37 °C, which is your body temperature.

Too much heat shakes the enzyme so hard that its shape changes. The active site no longer fits the substrate. We say the enzyme is denatured.

Think of frying an egg. Egg white is protein. Once it turns white and solid in the pan, you cannot turn it back. That is protein losing its shape for good.

📖 Read

pH tells you how acidic or alkaline something is. pH 2 is very acidic. pH 7 is neutral. Above 7 is alkaline.

Every enzyme has an optimum pH. The wrong pH changes the enzyme's shape, so the substrate no longer fits.

Amylase from your saliva works well in your mouth at about pH 7. When the food reaches the acid in your stomach, amylase stops working. Pepsin is different. It is a stomach enzyme, so it works best in strong acid.

Part 6

The sidekick

👀 Fix it

Some wingmen cannot work alone. This one is missing his sidekick.

Reactions: 0
📖 Read

Some enzymes need a non-protein helper before they can work. This helper is called a cofactor.

Minerals such as iron and zinc can be cofactors. Many vitamins are turned into helpers called coenzymes.

Your body cannot make these minerals, and it cannot make most vitamins. They have to come from your food. No cofactor means no working enzyme. This is one big reason a balanced diet matters.

Some wingmen only work when their partner is with them. The partner comes from what you eat.

Part 7

Saboteurs

👀 Experiment

The enzyme is working nicely. Now send in a saboteur and watch the speed. Try both.

Speed: 0 reactions per 10 s
📖 Read

A substance that slows down or stops an enzyme is called an inhibitor.

🎭 The fake key has nearly the same shape as the substrate. It sits in the active site and blocks it, so the real substrate cannot get in. This is a competitive inhibitor, because it competes with the substrate for the active site.

🤚 The arm-grabber does not go into the active site at all. It attaches somewhere else on the enzyme and bends it out of shape, so the active site no longer fits. This is a non-competitive inhibitor.

✅ Quick check
👀 In real life: medicine

Many medicines work by switching off an enzyme on purpose.

💊

Pain tablets

Aspirin and ibuprofen block an enzyme your body uses to make the chemicals that cause pain and swelling. Enzyme blocked, less pain.

🦠

Antibiotics

Penicillin blocks an enzyme that bacteria need to build their cell walls. Without a wall, the bacteria burst and die.

☠️ In real life: poison

Bitter cassava

Raw bitter cassava releases cyanide when it is grated or chewed. Cyanide shuts down an enzyme inside your cells that you need for respiration, the reaction that releases energy from glucose using oxygen.

Every cell in your body needs that energy all the time. With the enzyme switched off, cells cannot use oxygen to get energy, even while you are still breathing. Heart and brain cells are hit first. That is why cyanide poisoning is so fast and so dangerous.

🥔 Grate▸💧 Squeeze out the juice▸🚿 Wash▸🔥 Cook well

This is why bitter cassava is grated, squeezed, washed and cooked before eating, and why the juice is boiled for a long time to make cassareep. Those old kitchen steps remove the poison. Our grandparents were doing real science.

Note: cyanide works differently from the fake key and the arm-grabber you just saw. It is another example of a poison that shuts an enzyme down.

📖 The wingman's rules

Ça y est! Everything you learned, in six lines.

1

Enzymes are biological catalysts. They are proteins that speed up reactions by lowering the activation energy.

2

They are not used up. One enzyme works again and again.

3

Shape is everything. The substrate's shape must be complementary to the active site.

4

More collisions, faster reactions, until every active site is busy.

5

Heat and wrong pH change the shape. The enzyme becomes denatured.

6

Some need a cofactor from food. Inhibitors switch them off. That links enzymes to nutrition, medicine and poison.
